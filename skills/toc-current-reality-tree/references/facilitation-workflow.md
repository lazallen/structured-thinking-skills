# CRT facilitation workflow

Work through these stages in order. Ask **one focused question at a time**. Mirror the emerging tree back before extending it.

## Contents

1. Intake
2. Shape the tree
3. Scrutinise the logic
4. Hunt cycles and dilemmas
5. Read-aloud sweep
6. Close: review, dilemma offer, approval
7. Hand-offs to other methods

## 1. Intake

1. Open with: "Describe what you think is going on."
2. Capture fragments freely. Expect them to land in the **middle** of the eventual tree: people usually see the immediate problems first, while the long-term effect on the business is not yet obvious.
3. Write each fragment as an entity (one complete, present-tense sentence) and note its evidence type: observed, reported, inferred, or assumption.
4. If the situation is a past incident, confirm the conditions that produced it still hold. If they do, diagnose it like any current problem.
5. Do not jump to solutions or proposed changes.

## 2. Shape the tree

Messy capture is normal. Once there are enough pieces, re-anchor in two passes.

**UDE revision pass (upward).** The UDEs people start with are rarely the ones that matter in the end; they are often too local or at the wrong level. For each candidate ask:

- Is it observable, and does it exist now (or keep recurring)?
- Is it bad judged against a goal the people involved share? If people disagree, agree the goal before going further.
- Is it written as a condition, not as a missing solution ("lack of X")?
- Does something sit above it that matters more to the organisation? If so, that may be the real UDE.

**Root pass (downward).** From the UDEs, keep asking "What causes this?" toward root causes.

- Read every arrow as "If [cause], then [effect]."
- When a cause is not enough alone, find its partner and draw an **AND**: "If [A] and [B], then [C]."
- When another cause could produce the same effect on its own, draw it as an **additional cause** with its own arrow.
- Record material assumptions behind an arrow, especially where it leaps over intermediate steps.
- **Stop digging** when changing a condition would remove or weaken *several* UDEs or shared paths. Resist the search for a single "core problem" and never stop at a person — look for the policy, practice, or behaviour behind them.

Keep proportion: most of the tree is these sufficiency chains. Cycles and dilemmas (stage 4) are extra things to hunt, not replacements.

## 3. Scrutinise the logic

Scrutinise **every** link, starting with the ones that look weakest. Ask questions that let the human test their own claim rather than arguing with it. Keep the challenge fairly high but not relentless: one doubtful link at a time.

Use the **Categories of Legitimate Reservation (CLR)** as a shared, impersonal language for these questions:

| Category | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Clarity | Do we mean the same thing? Is the statement specific and understandable? |
| Entity existence | Is this condition actually true here, now? What's the evidence? |
| Causality existence | Does this cause really lead to that effect in this system? |
| Cause insufficiency | What else has to be true for this effect to happen? (Missing AND) |
| Additional cause | Could something else, on its own, produce the same effect? |
| Cause-effect reversal | Is the arrow backwards — does the "effect" actually cause the "cause"? |
| Predicted effect | If this cause were true, what else should we see? Do we see it? |
| Tautology | Does the explanation just restate the effect in other words? |

The first five do most of the work in a live conversation.

## 4. Hunt cycles and dilemmas

### Vicious cycles

Once a connected draft exists, ask whether anything higher in the tree feeds back into something lower down — for example, late deliveries cause expediting, which disrupts the schedule, which causes more late deliveries. When you find a loop:

- Draw the feedback arrow (thick; see the diagrams reference).
- Name breaking the loop as primary leverage. It usually matters more than any single bottom-most root cause.

### Embedded dilemmas

Watch for two opposing actions that each seem necessary — for example, "start new work as soon as it arrives" (to look responsive) against "limit work in progress" (to finish faster). Each action protects a different need, and both needs serve the same objective. While the conflict stays unresolved, the UDEs above it persist.

When you find one:

1. Surface it: name the objective, the two needs, and the two conflicting actions.
2. Rank it in the tree as a likely leverage cause.
3. Leave resolving it until stage 6. Don't switch methods mid-diagnosis.

A dilemma on its own is never a finished diagnosis; it belongs inside a scrutinised tree.

## 5. Read-aloud sweep

Near the end of a draft, go through every arrow methodically, bottom to top:

1. Read it back as a sentence: "If [cause] (and [partner]), then [effect]."
2. Ask the human to confirm, correct, or reject it.
3. Grade it **weak / plausible / solid**. Offer a 1–5 confidence score instead if the human prefers more resolution. Grades are subjective judgements; say so rather than presenting them as measurements.
4. Mark weak arrows dashed on the diagram and record the grade in the `%%` evidence comment.

Then list the weak links together as the open questions to check with people who know the system.

## 6. Close: review, dilemma offer, approval

1. Mirror the whole tree back: UDEs, main chains, loops, dilemmas, root causes, and the weakest links.
2. If an embedded dilemma was ranked as a leverage cause, **offer** dilemma work now that the tree has been reviewed. Explain why it fits: the conflict is between two actions that each protect something important, so forcing one side tends to fail. The TOC method for this is the **Evaporating Cloud**, which lays out the objective, both needs, and both actions, then surfaces the assumptions behind each link so that one can be challenged. This skill does not run that method.
3. Ask for approval before the tree is sent or shared with anyone.

## 7. Hand-offs to other methods

These are separate methods. Suggest them when the signal appears; do not blend them into the tree.

| Signal | Suggest |
| --- | --- |
| A ranked dilemma, and the human wants to resolve it | **Evaporating Cloud** — surfaces the assumptions behind a conflict so it can be broken |
| Both sides of a tension stay necessary over time | **Polarity management** — manage the tension rather than solve it |
| A proposed change needs testing before it is made | **Future Reality Tree** — checks whether a change produces the desired effects, and what it could break |
| The tree needs to be shared neutrally with people who were not in the room | **Communication Current Reality Tree** — presents the scrutinised conflict without blame |
| A person-facing feedback conversation is needed | A feedback method such as **Radical Candor** |
